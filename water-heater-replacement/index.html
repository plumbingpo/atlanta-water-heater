How do you know it is time to replace a water heater rather than repair it? Start with the age. Tanks tend to wear out between eight and twelve years, and the serial number on the label dates yours precisely. Then look at the symptoms. A leak from the tank itself, rusty water from the hot side only, and loud sediment noise all point to a tank near the end. A thermocouple, an element, or a relief valve can be swapped on almost any heater; a rusted-out tank cannot, and plenty of households in Atlanta, GA usually find that out the hard way, with a wet floor.

Replacing a water heater is a chance to get the next one right. Atlanta Water Heater sizes the new unit to your household, not just to the label on the old one. For tanks, that means looking at the first-hour rating, the amount of hot water the heater can deliver in a busy hour, rather than tank gallons alone. With tankless, it is flow rate in gallons per minute at the temperature rise your water needs in winter. We walk you through the real trade-offs between gas and electric, tank and tankless, and standard and heat pump models, in plain language and without pressure, and we confirm that your gas line, venting, and electrical service can support the choice before you commit.

The swap itself follows a checklist, not a guess: old tank drained and removed, fresh connectors and a working shutoff put in, gas lines leak-tested and fitted with a sediment trap, venting sized and pitched to the manufacturer's spec, and the relief valve piped to a safe discharge point. Where local code calls for it, we add a thermal expansion tank, a drain pan, or seismic straps, and we pull the permit when one is required. Before we leave, we fill and purge the tank, confirm draft and check for carbon monoxide on gas units, set the temperature near 120 degrees, and walk you through the new heater. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

Most tanks announce trouble weeks or months ahead with rumbling noises, rusty water, or hot showers that keep getting shorter. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • Loud rumbling or popping that flushing no longer fixes
  • A heater older than most tanks last, usually eight to twelve years
  • Hot water running out faster every month
  • Rust or corrosion on the tank, fittings, or vent connector
  • Repeated service calls on the same aging heater
  • Water leaking from the bottom or body of the tank
  • Discolored hot water, a sign the tank is corroding inside
  • Operating costs climbing on an older, less efficient heater

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Consider the vent first. A gas water heater's exhaust contains carbon monoxide, and a vent that is undersized, poorly pitched, or loosely connected can let it spill back into your home. Add a gas connection that has to be leak-free, an electrical circuit that has to match the new heater's load, and a relief valve that has to carry the right rating with a discharge line that is never capped or undersized, and there is a lot to get right. Local code may also call for an expansion tank, drain pan, or seismic straps, and some areas want a permit. Switching to tankless or a heat pump adds new gas line sizing, vent materials, circuits, and condensate lines to the list.

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

A few preventive steps can help you avoid a cold shower or a wet floor down the road. Here are our recommendations:

  • Note the install date and keep up with maintenance so the next replacement is never an emergency
  • Descale a tankless unit every year, or more often with hard water
  • Avoid running the tank hotter than about 120 degrees unless there is a specific need
  • Keep household water pressure in a safe range to protect the tank and its fittings
  • Have the anode rod checked around year three to five and replaced if it is worn
  • Clean the air filter on a heat pump water heater every few months
  • Flush the new tank once or twice a year to keep sediment from building up
