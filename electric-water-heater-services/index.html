Electric water heaters fail in a few very specific ways, and the symptom usually tells you where to look. No hot water anywhere suggests a tripped breaker, a tripped high-limit reset, or a failed upper element or upper thermostat, because the upper side controls when the lower side gets power. A few minutes of hot water followed by lukewarm points to the lower element or lower thermostat. Water that comes out scalding points to a thermostat stuck closed or an element shorted to the tank, which is also what trips the red reset button. Those are the calls we answer most in Atlanta, GA, and nearly all of them are fixable.

Resetting a tripped red button or swapping an element blindly rarely solves the problem for long. Our approach in Atlanta homes is to test the whole circuit. We confirm the breaker and the 240-volt supply, measure the resistance of both elements, check each element for a short to ground, and verify that each thermostat opens and closes as it should. We also inspect the wiring at the junction box and thermostat terminals for heat damage. A high-limit that keeps tripping almost always has a reason, such as a thermostat stuck closed or a grounded element, and we find it so the button stays set.

The repair itself is usually the easy part. Elements, thermostats, and high-limit switches are standard parts, and our trucks carry the common sizes. Replacing an element means shutting off power, draining the tank, installing the new element, and refilling completely before power is restored, which protects the new element from a dry fire. We also repair damaged wiring, tighten or replace terminal connections, and flush sediment from the tank bottom. If replacement makes more sense, we install new electric heaters sized to your household's first-hour demand, confirm the breaker and wire gauge are correct, and add the expansion tank, drain pan, and strapping your local code requires. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. Keep an eye out for the following:

  • Rusty water, leaks around an element, or water at the base
  • A breaker that trips when the heater calls for heat
  • A red reset button that keeps tripping
  • No hot water at all from any faucet
  • Tap water much hotter than the thermostat setting
  • Browned wiring or a burnt odor from behind the access cover
  • A short burst of hot water, then nothing but tepid
  • Crackling noises as the heater runs, often from sediment

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

A typical electric water heater sits on a dedicated 240-volt, 30-amp circuit, which is reason enough not to learn on it. The thermostats sit right next to live terminals behind the access panel, and one slipped probe can start a dangerous arc. Replacing an element also means draining the tank, and forgetting to refill it completely means the new element burns out the moment power returns. Many problems overlap, too. A tripped reset can come from a stuck thermostat, a grounded element, or a loose connection overheating a terminal, so guessing often replaces the wrong part.

Professional service gets you the right fix the first time and a safe heater afterward. Rather than guess, we meter each element and thermostat, check for shorts to the tank, and inspect every wire and terminal for heat damage. Repairs are done with the tank drained and refilled correctly, and we verify amp draw once power is back on. When replacement makes more sense, we confirm the breaker size and wire gauge match the new heater and install the expansion tank, drain pan, or seismic straps your code requires. The result is dependable hot water without surprises.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Set both thermostats near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
  • Replace a worn anode rod before corrosion moves on to the tank itself
  • In hard water areas, a softener or low-watt-density elements can extend element life
  • Never turn the breaker back on until the tank is completely full of water
  • Lift the T&P valve lever yearly to make sure it opens and reseats
  • Flush sediment from the tank once or twice a year so it cannot bury the lower element
  • If the reset button trips, have it checked rather than pressing it over and over
