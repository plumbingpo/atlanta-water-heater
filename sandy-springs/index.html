How Sandy Springs Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

Routine maintenance makes all the difference between a water heater that lasts and one that fails early. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: a yearly flush, an anode inspection every few years, a yearly test of the T&P valve, a temperature setting around 120 degrees, and annual descaling for tankless units on hard water, so small issues get caught early.

How old is your water heater? The serial number on the label usually encodes the manufacture date, and its age tells you a lot. Most tank models reach 8 to 12 years, heat pump models 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units around 20. If your heater is past that point and also shows rusty water, rumbling, or dampness under the tank, it's usually due for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and less stressful than a rushed emergency install after a leak.

Minor issues don't stay minor for long. Consider a spent anode rod: replacing it is a short, affordable repair, but if nobody replaces it, the water starts eating the tank steel, and just like that a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to save our number in their phone and reach out at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and prevents a flooded utility room.

Hot Water Headaches Sandy Springs Homeowners Face

Around Sandy Springs, water heater trouble tends to trace back to a handful of causes. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be a real factor: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why annual descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Age matters just as much. Many homes in the area still have the original tank, and once a tank is past about ten years, the anode rod is usually spent and corrosion begins attacking the steel from the inside. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from failed thermocouples and draft problems to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Well water may bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water often means a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older installations often need code updates when a heater is replaced: a thermal expansion tank where a check valve or pressure regulator creates a closed system, a correctly piped T&P discharge line, a drain pan where a leak would hit finished floors, and seismic straps where local code requires them. All of these is fixable, and most are much cheaper when caught early. If something seems off, a diagnostic visit from our team can keep a small problem from turning into a flooded floor.
