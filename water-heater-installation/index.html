Many of the installation calls we get in Atlanta, GA are not replacements at all. A family adds a bathroom and needs more capacity. A homeowner wants the heater out of a hall closet and into the garage or basement. A remodel calls for a second unit at the far end of the house, or an all-electric conversion means trading gas for a heat pump model. Every one of them involves new work: gas lines that must be sized for the load, vents that must be routed and pitched correctly, circuits that must match the heater, and water lines that must be run cleanly. That is the planning Atlanta Water Heater does before any installation begins.

Before any pipe is cut, we match the heater to your household. For tanks, first-hour rating counts more than gallons; for tankless, it is gallons per minute at your winter temperature rise. Next, we choose the spot together. A gas model has to vent outdoors and draw enough combustion air. A heat pump model has to have breathing room, temperatures above roughly 40 degrees, and a condensate drain. Electric tankless models often require a dedicated high-amperage circuit. And wherever a leak could damage finished space, a drain pan goes underneath. Clear choices and a firm price come before any work.

The final stretch is where a careful installer and a fast one look nothing alike. Our technicians leak-test every gas joint, set vent pitch and clearances to the manufacturer's spec, match the circuit and breaker to the heater, and use dielectric unions or fittings where copper meets steel. We pipe the relief valve discharge full size to the right spot, install code-required expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps, and handle the permit if needed. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. Keep an eye out for the following:

  • A tank that cannot keep up with your household anymore
  • A tank in a tight spot that is hard to service or replace
  • Taps at the far end of the house that take minutes to warm up
  • Remodel plans that add fixtures or a second bathroom
  • A property where the water heater has to go in from scratch
  • A large soaking tub that a standard tank cannot fill
  • An older install without an expansion tank, pan, or straps where required
  • A planned switch from gas to electric, or to a heat pump model

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Consider the gas side first. A new heater on an undersized line starves for fuel whenever the furnace or range is running, and a whole-house tankless unit often needs a larger line than the house already has. Vent material, size, pitch, and clearances all have to be right, or exhaust drifts back inside. Electric models bring their own math, since breaker, wire gauge, and panel capacity all have to match. Add relief valve piping, dielectric fittings, expansion tanks, drain pans, seismic straps, and permit rules that vary from one jurisdiction to the next, and there are a lot of details to get right.

What you get from a pro is an installation that is done once. Our technicians plan the gas line, vent, and circuit before the first cut, take care of the permit when your jurisdiction requires one, and install the relief valve discharge and code items correctly. We test every joint, check every gas install for draft and carbon monoxide, and set the temperature low enough to prevent scalding. Hurried installs leave behind undersized gas lines, poor vent pitch, and missing expansion tanks. Ours are built to pass inspection and to last. And if the simpler option is the right one for your home, we say so plainly.

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

A few simple habits, like an annual flush and a quick look at the relief valve, can add years to a tank. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Moisture in the drain pan or at the relief line is an early warning worth a call
  • Store paint, gasoline, and solvents far from any gas water heater
  • Have the anode rod inspected every few years to protect the tank
  • Drain a few gallons from a new tank twice a year to control sediment
  • Do not box the heater in; leave room for service and airflow
  • Set the thermostat near 120 degrees for safety and efficiency
  • Plan an annual descaling if you installed a tankless unit
